1. The short answer

Prose does not stop either one. A check that runs does, and it has never once been bypassed. The batching rule lived in CLAUDE.md as a hard gate for three months and produced a 6% compliance rate. Meanwhile no override marker has ever been created on this machine: no ~/.claude/*.allow, no .opus_ok, no completion.allow_stop. When a gate refuses, the work gets fixed. The mechanism is sound, so the remaining problem is three specific defects in how the gating is wired.

3. The leak: a spent budget lets a failing session exit

4 of 13 sessions had exhausted the shared 4-refusal budget. When the budget is spent, the gates stop blocking. Each of those sessions therefore ended with its refusal reason unresolved, which is the precise failure the gates exist to prevent. The cap itself is correct: the Stop chain does not short-circuit, and _stop_budget.py records that on one event the gates at chain positions 5, 6, 8 and 13 all blocked, so a per-gate ceiling of two would have admitted up to 26 extra turns. The cap has to be shared. Its side effect is a hard exit after four tries.

A bigger cap only buys more turns of the same argument. The way out is to spend fewer refusals on causes that need no judgement, so the budget is still there for the ones that do.

5. The false-positive tax

A refusal caused by a gate misreading its input is waste wearing the costume of rigor. In this session the completion gate refused four times on its "asserted claim contradicted by its own evidence" rule, and every one of the four was the claim parser reading a number out of prose: sha256 as the count 256, "540 of 9,009" and "12 of 126" as shortfalls that no count record can satisfy, and "13 in the trash" as a deletion. The rule behind it is right and worth keeping. The trigger misfires on ordinary English, and until today nothing counted that.

The established practice for this is called guardrail telemetry, where every check emits a verdict and the false-positive rate is computed from labeled traffic. One published interceptor reports 1.2% on 500 benign tool calls. Nothing here emitted a verdict, so the rate was unknown and unknowable.

7. The finding that indicts this page

Every mechanism on this page costs about 52 ms of Python interpreter startup, and the three nudges added while writing it made each Bash call roughly 156 ms slower. That was measured only after Brandon asked for more honesty, which is the wrong order. A research page about stopping waste, produced by adding waste, is worth saying out loud. 
hook latency on ONE Bash call     695 ms across 12 processes
a do-nothing python hook           51.8 ms   <- the floor every hook pays
therefore interpreter startup      622 ms
all twelve hooks' logic combined   ~13 ms
the three nudges added today      ~156 ms, almost entirely startup

after consolidating those three into one process:
processes per Bash call            12 -> 11
measured saving                    22 ms   (the prediction was 52 ms)
my nudges still cost              ~104 ms per Bash call

The consolidation was proved by equivalence rather than by fresh fixtures: every original control was run through both the old script and the new entry point, and all 33 paired cases returned identical verdicts, 40 of 40 correct overall. That is a stronger test than the ones below it on this page, and it is still author-written.

The controls on this page are weak evidence, and here is the proof

Every "N of N controls" figure above was written by the same author as the code it tests, in the same hour. A fixture cannot catch a misunderstanding it shares with the code. The demonstration is on this page already: batch_streak_gate.py passed 5 of 5 of its own controls and was still wrong, because every fixture counted tool calls per JSONL record exactly as the buggy code did. The bug surfaced only when an outside document described the real record-split behaviour. Treat those counts as evidence that the logic matches its author's intent, and as nothing more.

None of the six hooks has fired once in production. They are not loaded in the session that wrote them, so the honest status is written and self-tested, unverified in use. claude_nudges.py now appends one line per firing to ~/.claude/_state/nudge_ledger.jsonl, which starts at zero, so the question of whether they ever fire and whether they are right when they do becomes answerable. The three test firings were moved aside rather than removed.

8. What is still open, and why I did not do it

The claim parser needs the fix, and I am the wrong one to make it. The parser lives inside completion_stop_gate.py, the gate that judges whether my claims are honest. A model quietly editing the rule that grades it has an obvious conflict of interest, however correct the patch looks. The defect is reported with its four instances and the remedy is small: skip fenced code spans, and do not read a digit sequence inside a token like sha256 as a quantity. That is Brandon's call to make or to delegate.

Three further items are stated here rather than quietly dropped. Nine blocking safety gates still run one process each, about 470 ms per Bash call, and consolidating them behind one dispatcher would recover most of it; that one was left alone because a bug in such a dispatcher drops a refusal silently, where a bug in the nudge dispatcher only costs time. The 4 refusals with no reason recorded mean the dispatcher drops the cause on one path, which should be traced before anyone trusts the cause table as complete. And the labeled tally above comes from one session of labeling, so the false-positive rate it prints is a floor rather than a measurement.
