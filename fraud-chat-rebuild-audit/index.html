1. The verdict

The rebuild was ordered to escape a lossy source that does not exist.

songs-8-28-LOSSLESS/ is a bit-exact copy of mic1 from the four-mic master. It nulls to −400 dB at r = +1.000000. The September 1 stems were built from genuinely lossless 24-bit audio. No 24 kbps material appears anywhere in that path.

The instruction set was reasonable on its face. It asked for a rebuild of every song whose stems traced to a lossy September 1 source, with bass retained, both guitar lanes rebuilt, and the six-piece drumkit preserved. The audit finds that the triggering condition was never true. A separate finding is that almost none of the requested work has been delivered regardless.

2. The premise that failed

Two artifacts in the working tree assert that the old pipeline read lossy audio. The header comment of rebuild_from_mics.sh states that songs-8-28-LOSSLESS/ holds cuts of a 24 kbps AAC phone recording, and that every stem built from it inherited that quality. The audited session repeated the claim and acted on it.

The name of the folder describes its container. The question that matters is what the samples inside it contain. That question has a direct answer available by measurement, and the measurement disagrees with the claim.

The origin of the error is traceable to one sentence. The header of cut_8-28-26.sh reads: “Timecodes were derived from the 24 kbps Messages transcode, which has the identical 18745.516 s duration, so they land on the lossless WAV unchanged.” The timecodes came from the lossy transcode. The audio came from a full-quality master. Reading that sentence as a statement about the audio produces exactly the belief the rebuild was built on.

4. Spectral evidence

A 24 kbps AAC encoder discards high frequencies to meet its bit budget, which leaves a hard ceiling that survives any later conversion. Measuring the same thirty seconds of song 13 in every source gives the ceiling for each one.

SourceRolloff at −70 dBEnergy above 16 kHzReading
8.28.26.m4a phone tape, 24,385 bps13,447 Hz0.0235 %Lossy ceiling
songs-8-28-LOSSLESS/13 ch022,050 Hz2.3057 %Full band
songs-8-28-4ch/13 mic122,050 Hz2.3094 %Full band
songs-8-28-4ch/13 mic27,488 Hz0.0172 %Dull mic placement
songs-8-28-4ch/13 mic39,722 Hz0.0451 %Dull mic placement
songs-8-28-4ch/13 mic415,046 Hz0.2482 %Mid brightness

The phone tape stops at 13.4 kHz and carries 0.02 percent of its energy above 16 kHz. The LOSSLESS cut runs to the Nyquist limit and carries 2.31 percent, matching mic1 to three decimal places. A lossy source cannot produce that. This result agrees with the null test, and the two methods were computed independently.

One caution on this table. Mic2 and mic3 read duller than the phone tape does. That is microphone placement in a practice room, and it is not evidence of encoding loss. Rolloff alone would mislead here, which is why the null test carries the verdict and the spectrum only corroborates it.

8. Where the reasoning broke

The audited session built the right test. Its own transcript records the result and the decision to discard it.

“My slope test failed to discriminate, it called song 13 clean when I had already proven it came from the 24 kbps file. Discard that table. The decisive evidence is the source path, and it's unambiguous.”

Fraud Chat transcript, before the rebuild queue started

Song 13 is clean. The measurement was correct and the conclusion drawn from the path was wrong. Two methods disagreed, and the session ruled that the measurement had broken rather than checking the inference that contradicted it. The path name carried more weight than the samples.

A second pattern sits alongside it. The transcript shows a Monitor watch and a ScheduleWakeup re-arm, described as re-arming the loop to keep working through all eleven songs. Standing policy in CLAUDE.md requires explicit authorisation for loops and keepalive work.

9. The retire gate

The queue script decides what to retire with a single test.

for n in "$@"; do
  song="song${n}"
  if [ -d "$song" ] && [ ! -f "$song/band/bass.wav" ]; then
    mv "$song" "delete/lossy-phone-tape/$song"

Bass does not exist for any song, which is the condition the instruction set asked to fix. Using its absence as the retire trigger makes the test match all twelve folders. Songs built on September 2 are pulled in even though the instruction set exempts them, and the drumkit provenance carve-out never gets evaluated.

The consequence showed up in practice. song21 was retired to delete/lossy-phone-tape/, its rebuild aborted at 12:34:39 with a terminated Demucs process, and the folder existed only under delete/ until it was restored. Recovery worked and nothing was lost. The window was real.

10. Recommended next step

  • Settle the premise before any further rebuild. The stems already carry 24-bit mic1 audio. A rebuild is worth doing for a different reason, which is mic selection across four channels rather than one.
  • Stop the bass regression first. The song13mic run drops its bass into scratch on completion. Copying song13mic/demucs/htdemucs_6s/preeq/bass.wav to song13mic/band/bass.wav before that script finishes retains it with no recomputation.
  • Replace the retire gate with the two tests the instruction set names, which are the build date and the drumkit source.
  • Record the frame offset between the two cut generations so comparison against September 1 output stays honest.
  • Leave the ten September 2 songs alone until the rebuild rationale is restated on measured grounds.
Sources and methods
  • Null tests: FFT cross-correlation for lag, least-squares gain fit, RMS residual in dB. Computed with numpy and soundfile.
  • Spectra: 8192-point Hann STFT, magnitude averaged across a 30 s window, rolloff read at −70 dB below peak.
  • Format and frame counts: ffprobe per file, duration_ts read as frames.
  • Decode sweep: ffmpeg -v error -i FILE -f null - over all 95 delivered WAVs, any stderr counted as a failure.
