Sorcery for Magic Players

Ten rules apiece, in five groups, ahead of your first game of Sorcery: Contested Realm

Every rule below comes from the official December 2025 rulebook, the one rewritten to match the Gothic release. Where a claim comes from somewhere else, the line says so.

The comparisons assume you already play Magic. That knowledge helps you about half the time, and the other half it actively misleads you. The items marked in rust are the ones that catch Magic players out.

Group 1. The Board and the Two Decks

The physical setup is the first shock. Sorcery is a board game wearing a card game's clothes.

  1. There is a board, and it is five squares by four. Twenty squares, all of them empty void at the start. You and your opponent fill them in as you play. Magic: your permanents sit in an abstract zone with no geography. In Sorcery, where a card sits decides what it can reach, what can reach it, and often whether a spell is legal at all. Rulebook, Areas of the Realm, page 12
  2. You bring two decks, not one. The Atlas holds your sites. The Spellbook holds everything else. Magic: lands and spells share one 60-card pile, and that pile decides your game. Sorcery splits them so the two resources are independent. Rulebook, Building Your Own Decks, page 26
  3. Every turn you choose which deck to draw from. Step 4 of your start phase is "draw a card from either your spellbook or atlas." You pick. Magic: mana screw and mana flood are the game's oldest complaints. This single rule deletes both. Short on land, draw the Atlas. Short on action, draw the Spellbook. Rulebook, Turn Summary, Start Phase, page 16
  4. Your Avatar is a card on the board with life and attack power. It has a name, a starting life that doubles as its maximum, and an attack rating. Magic: you are an abstract life total. In Sorcery you are a physical unit standing somewhere, and it can swing. Rulebook, Avatars, page 5
  5. Sites are your lands, and you place them on specific squares. Playing a site replaces a void square. Sites build into land masses and bodies of water. Magic: a Forest is a Forest wherever it sits. A Sorcery site is a piece of terrain with a position, and position is a resource. Rulebook, Sites, page 12
  6. Minions summon onto your own sites, and you choose which one. A cast minion appears "atop any of your sites." Magic: a creature enters the battlefield and that is the whole story. Here, summoning is also a placement decision. Rulebook, Casting Minions, page 19
  7. Any number of minions can stand on one site. The rulebook allows it and warns against it in the same breath, calling it "rarely a good idea to concentrate your forces." Magic: there is no stacking question at all. Here, a pile of minions on one square is a target for anything that hits a location. Rulebook, Casting Minions, page 19
  8. The void eats anything that walks into it. A minion that ends up in the void is banished, removed from the game, unless it has Voidwalk. Magic: exile is a card effect you cast. Here it is terrain, and the board starts as nothing else. Rulebook, The Void, page 12
  9. Sites have a basement. Every site has a surface and a subsurface. Under land it is underground. Under water it is underwater. Magic: the battlefield is flat. Sorcery gives you three usable regions plus the void, each with its own adjacency. Rulebook, Surface and Subsurface, page 13
  10. Copy limits are four, three, two and one. Ordinary allows four, exceptional three, elite two, unique exactly one. Magic: four of anything that is not basic land. Sorcery bakes deck limits into rarity itself, so a rare card is rare in your deck too. Rulebook, Rarity, page 26

Group 2. Mana and Threshold

Two separate resources that look like one thing in Magic.

  1. Sites give mana automatically, all of them, every turn. At the start of your turn every site you control provides one mana. Magic: you tap lands one at a time as you spend. Here the pool fills itself and you spend down from it. Rulebook, Gaining Mana, page 17
  2. Unspent mana disappears at end of turn. You gain mana only on your own turn and lose whatever you did not use. Magic: mana empties at each step and phase. Same principle, and it bites harder here, since you cannot hold mana up for a response. Rulebook, Mana, page 17
  3. A site pays you the moment it lands. "When one of your sites enters the realm, it immediately provides one mana." Magic: a tapped land does nothing this turn. Every Sorcery site is effectively an untapped land the turn you play it. Rulebook, Gaining Mana, page 17
  4. Threshold is a second cost, and you never spend it. Elemental threshold is a minimum affinity you must already have. Using it costs nothing. Magic: the closest thing is devotion, and devotion only powers a few cards. In Sorcery, threshold gates most of the card pool. Rulebook, Elemental Threshold, page 18
  5. Only your own sites count toward threshold. The rulebook is explicit: "your opponent's Aqueduct does not grant you affinity." Magic: there is no equivalent question. Worth stating because the board is shared and it looks like it should count. Rulebook, Elemental Threshold, page 18
  6. A two-element card is a card of both elements. Sir Pelleas needs one Air and two Water, and he counts as an Air spell and a Water spell at once. Magic: gold cards work the same way for color identity. The familiar part. Rulebook, Multiple Element Threshold, page 18
  7. Artifacts mostly ignore threshold entirely. "All artifacts and most abilities have no threshold at all." Magic: artifacts are colorless and castable in any deck. The instinct carries over cleanly. Rulebook, Elemental Threshold, page 18
  8. Losing a site does not claw back the mana it already gave. The mana it provided this turn stays in your pool. Magic: land destruction in response to a tap is a real play pattern. That trick does not exist here. Rulebook, Gaining Mana, page 17
  9. Stealing a site pays you nothing until next turn. A site you take control of during your turn provides no mana until your following turn. Magic: a stolen untapped land is usable immediately. Different here, and it is a real tempo consideration. Rulebook, Gaining Mana, page 17
  10. You cannot short-pay a cost. "You cannot pay a mana cost if" you lack the mana. There is no partial cast. Magic: identical. Listed because the two-pool system tempts you to think otherwise. Rulebook, Using Mana, page 17

Group 3. Turns and the Storyline

This is the group that breaks Magic instincts hardest. Read it twice.

  1. There is no stack. There is a Storyline. The word "stack" appears zero times in the official rulebook. The word "instant" appears zero times as well. Magic: the stack is the spine of the game. Throw the word away and learn the new one. Verified by text search of the December 2025 rulebook, 12,646 words
  2. The Storyline resolves in order, front to back. Events are placed on it and "are then resolved in order, one at a time." Magic: the stack is last in, first out. The Storyline is the opposite. Everything you know about ordering is inverted. Rulebook, The Storyline, page 31
  3. The non-active player places events first. When events compete for the same point, the non-active player places theirs, then the active player places after them. Magic: APNAP puts the active player's triggers on the stack first, so the non-active player's resolve first. Sorcery reaches the same outcome by the opposite route. Rulebook, Triggered Abilities, page 31
  4. Triggers cut in line at the current moment. A triggered ability inserts a new event right where the story is now, "temporarily interrupting any remaining events." Magic: triggers go on top of the stack and wait. Here they resolve immediately at the point of the trigger, then the story resumes. Rulebook, Triggered Abilities, page 31
  5. Targets get locked when the event goes on, not when it resolves. "Target units or locations must be chosen as an event is being added to the Storyline." Magic: same principle for targeting on announcement. The familiar part of an unfamiliar system. Rulebook, The Storyline, page 31
  6. Your interaction on their turn is Defend and Intercept. Defend lets you tap to move in and join a fight. Intercept lets you force combat on an enemy that moved without attacking. Magic: you hold up counterspells and removal. Here your interaction is positional and it is declared with units already on the board. Rulebook, Defend page 24, Intercept page 25
  7. Three phases, and that is the whole turn. Start, Main, End. No upkeep, no draw step as a separate thing, no combat phase. Magic: untap, upkeep, draw, main, combat, main, end, cleanup. Sorcery folds combat into the main phase entirely. Rulebook, Turn Summary, page 16
  8. Attacking happens in your main phase, any time, any order. Units act "as many times as you have resources to do so, and in any order you like." Move a minion, cast a spell, move another minion. Magic: combat is a walled-off phase with steps. Losing that structure is liberating and disorienting at once. Rulebook, Main Phase, page 16
  9. Minion damage wipes at end of turn. Avatars do not heal. Step 2 of the end phase removes all damage from minions. The rulebook adds a note that Avatars work differently. Magic: creature damage clears at cleanup and players do not heal. Identical instinct, and it transfers. Rulebook, End Phase, page 16
  10. Summoning sickness blocks tapping, and only tapping. A fresh minion "cannot tap, or be tapped, to pay for costs associated with any ability." Magic: summoning sickness stops attacking and tap abilities. Here it is defined purely as a tapping restriction, which is narrower than it sounds. Rulebook, Summoning Sickness, page 19

Group 4. Combat and Killing

One number does two jobs, and killing a player takes two hits.

  1. Power is attack and defense at the same time. A minion's power rating is what it deals and what it can take. Magic: power and toughness are separate numbers you learn as a pair. Sorcery collapses them into one, so a 3 is a 3/3. Rulebook, Minions, page 7
  2. Some minions do split the number. Certain minions show attack power beside a sword and defense power beside a shield. Magic: this is the case where Sorcery looks like Magic again. Treat the split number as the exception. Rulebook, Advanced Concepts, page 27
  3. Fighting is simultaneous. When units clash they strike each other at the same time, each dealing damage equal to power. Magic: combat damage is also simultaneous. The instinct holds. Rulebook, Attacking the Enemy, page 22
  4. You must be standing on the same square to attack. "You may only attack enemy units or the surface of enemy sites that are at your location." Magic: any creature can attack any opponent from anywhere. In Sorcery, reach is a movement problem first and a combat problem second. Rulebook, Attacking the Enemy, page 22
  5. Move and Attack is one action, in that order. You tap once. Movement resolves fully, then you declare and resolve the attack. Magic: declaring attackers is a single simultaneous step. Here it is a sequence, and the order is fixed. Rulebook, Move and Attack, page 21
  6. Charge is haste. "This unit can tap, or be tapped, to pay for costs associated with any ability on the turn it is summoned." Magic: haste, worded as the exact inverse of the summoning sickness rule. Rulebook, Common Keyword Abilities, page 37
  7. Lethal is deathtouch. "Any strictly positive amount of damage this card deals to a minion is enough to kill." Magic: deathtouch, one for one. Note it says minion, so it does not shortcut an Avatar. Rulebook, Common Keyword Abilities, page 38
  8. Striking first is first strike. A unit with this keyword lands its blow while the target is still winding up. Several of them go together. Magic: first strike, including the double-first-strike stacking behaviour. Rulebook, Striking First, page 23
  9. Hitting an undefended site damages the Avatar and cannot finish them. Site attack damage reduces the controlling Avatar's life. Site damage does not count as direct damage, so it cannot deliver the death blow. Magic: unblocked creatures walk the defender down to zero. Sorcery makes chip damage real and caps what it can accomplish. Rulebook, Attacking the Enemy, page 22
  10. Killing takes two steps: death's door, then a death blow. Zero life puts an Avatar at death's door. Only a further blow ends it. Magic: zero life is the game. Here it is a warning shot, and your opponent gets a turn to answer it. Rulebook, How to Win, page 3

Group 5. Deckbuilding, Keywords and Culture

What to build, what the words mean, and what the game is like socially.

  1. Sixty spells, thirty sites, one Avatar. Spellbook at least 60, Atlas at least 30, exactly one Avatar. December 2025 set the spellbook number at 60. Magic: 60 total with roughly 24 lands. Sorcery asks for 90 cards plus a commander-like Avatar. Rulebook, Building Your Own Decks, page 26
  2. Your Avatar is free of the deck count. It does not count toward either total and is not part of either deck. Magic: a commander sits outside the 99 the same way. The closest structural analogy in the whole game. Rulebook, Rarity, page 26
  3. The Golden Rule is a real printed rule. "Some text is intentionally informal or necessarily compact. Use common sense and be cool." Magic: comprehensive rules run past 250 pages and a judge settles disputes. Sorcery hands the ruling to the two of you. Rulebook, The Golden Rule, page 5
  4. Movement keywords are the real keyword set. Airborne, Burrowing, Submerge and Voidwalk each unlock a region. Airborne also allows diagonal steps. Magic: flying is evasion. Airborne is evasion plus a movement grammar, because there is a map to move on. Rulebook, Common Keyword Abilities, pages 37 and 38
  5. Airborne units can only be hit by Airborne or Ranged. They cannot be targeted by attacks unless the attacker also has Airborne, and only Airborne or Ranged can intercept them. Magic: flying blocked by flying or reach. Nearly the same rule under another name. Rulebook, Airborne, page 37
  6. Stealth is stronger than hexproof and it burns out. Stealthy minions cannot be targeted, intercepted, defended against, or hit by projectiles. A stealth token tracks it, and the minion loses it on any interaction with the realm. Magic: hexproof is permanent and narrower. Stealth is broader and temporary, so it is a window rather than a shield. Rulebook, Stealth, page 37
  7. Genesis and Deathrite are enter-the-battlefield and dies triggers. Genesis fires when the card enters the realm. Deathrite fires when the unit dies, on its way to the cemetery. Magic: ETB and death triggers, renamed. Cemetery is the graveyard. Rulebook, Common Keyword Abilities, pages 37 and 38
  8. Disable is a full lockdown, not a tap. A disabled minion loses all abilities including Move and Attack, does not strike when fighting, and cannot cast. Magic: tapping a creature stops attacks. Disable is closer to Pacifism plus losing all abilities at once. Rulebook, Disable, page 37
  9. The precon box is four decks, and it is the right way in. Cardboard Guide puts the four-deck precon at forty to sixty dollars, one deck per element, playable with no deckbuilding. Magic: a preconstructed Commander deck serves one player. One Sorcery precon box seats four. Transcript corpus, Cardboard Guide, sVhKOGEoTBc and _N4L72gkAeM
  10. Booster boxes run past two hundred dollars right now. Live retailer check on 2026-09-10 found in-stock Gothic boxes at $209.97 and $219.99. Every listing under $180 was sold out. Magic: a Play Booster box sits well under that. Buy the precon, play a dozen games, then decide. ToyWiz and Game Nerdz, both http 200, checked 2026-09-10
