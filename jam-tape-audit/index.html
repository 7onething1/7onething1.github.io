1. Which tape has no frequency loss

Container and bitrate describe a file. They do not measure what reached the disk. The test here samples nine windows spread across each tape, averages the spectrum, and reads the level at 16 kHz against the 1 kHz to 4 kHz band that every one of these recordings has. A codec cutoff shows up as a wall, with energy tens of dB below anything an instrument produces.

One tape qualifies. band-practice-8-14-26.wav reads -38.7 dB at 16 kHz. The next best reads -76.8 dB. That is a gap of 38 dB, and the five AAC tapes sit 38 to 47 dB below the WAV.

Every other file in the set is a 23.6 kbps AAC voice recording, or a decode of one.

3. 7.31.26 carried the 8.28 failure

The old run kept 10 songs across a 169 minute tape. Song coverage measured 10.6 percent, which is the alarm and almost exactly the 10.7 percent the broken 8.28 run produced. The audit found 21 stretches with no take, totalling 44 minutes.

Ten of those stretches were sampled and transcribed. All ten returned zero words, against a talk control from the same tape that returned 37 words at 0.93 words per second.

Re-running the detector with --calib loudness, the same flag that repaired 8.28, took the tape from 10 songs to 35 songs and coverage from 10.6 percent to 63.6 percent. The audit then reported zero uncovered regions. Thirty of the 35 songs are ones the old run never held in any form.

Two songs from the old run do not appear whole in the new one, and both are accounted for. The take at 1:36:57 is now split across two songs that together cover the same audio. The take at 0:30:08 is a 53 second stretch the new run calls two fragments, which is the one genuine downgrade.

4. The 8-14 cut that shipped is short

The 8-14 detector run is healthy on its own terms. Coverage reads 71.6 percent, 80.3 percent counting every take, and the audit reports zero uncovered regions. The problem sits downstream of the detector.

The cut on disk came from the strict run, which holds 20 songs. The default run on the same tape holds 31. Mapping one onto the other, all 20 strict songs sit inside longer default takes, so the strict pass was clipping songs as well as dropping them.

Eleven songs in the default run have no counterpart in the shipped cut, at 0:10:24, 0:15:58, 0:20:21, 0:33:40, 0:38:39, 1:17:26, 1:25:46, 1:42:20, 2:05:36, 2:28:41 and 3:31:31.

The 8-14 tape is worth cutting again, since it is the only full band master in the set.

5. Two tapes still hold uncovered playing

Three tapes had never been through the song detector at all. Two of them come back with playing the new run still does not hold.

  • 5.8.26 reads 36.0 percent coverage across 20 songs. Six regions totalling 10 minutes measure as playing and carry no take. All six transcribed to zero words against a talk control that returned six words.
  • 7.3.26 reads 38.2 percent coverage across 17 songs. Seven regions totalling 12 minutes carry no take. All seven transcribed to zero words against a talk control that returned 20 words at 0.50 words per second.
  • 5-22-26 reads 81.7 percent coverage across 24 songs, the highest here. One region of two minutes carries no take.

On both of the tapes that fire the talk control at 0.0 percent, the audit test rejects conversation cleanly, so the zero-word readings in those regions carry their full weight.

6. The calibration flag decides every lossy tape

The 8.28 repair added --calib loudness, which drops pulse salience from the split that decides playing against not playing. That was treated as a fix for one tape. Running both calibrations across this whole set shows it is the correct setting for every phone recording here.

5-22-26 is the sharpest case. Under the default calibration it returns 8 songs at 7.1 percent coverage, the worst reading anywhere in this set. Under loudness it returns 24 songs at 81.7 percent, the best. The tape did not change between those two runs.

The split follows fidelity rather than session. Five lossy tapes all improve under loudness, by 19 to 75 percentage points. The one full band master is the only tape where the default calibration already reads correctly, at 71.6 percent. Pulse salience carries information when the recording has real high frequency content, and carries noise when a 23.6 kbps encoder has stripped the transient detail the autocorrelation depends on.

Standing rule this produces: run --calib loudness on any phone or voice memo band tape. Keep the default for full band masters, and check the tape's level at 16 kHz, then choose.

7. Method, controls, and limits

Each tape went through the same four steps. The profile is built from loudness and a mid-band ratio, deliberately not the detector's own feature set, so the audit does not inherit the blind spot it exists to find. Coverage calibrates on the detector's own confirmed songs rather than a hand-picked threshold. Every disputed region is then settled by transcription.

python3 scripts/profile_tape.py --audio TAPE --out profile.npz
python3 scripts/audit_coverage.py --profile profile.npz --manifest MANIFEST.json --talk START END
~/.venvs/rsf311/bin/python scripts/speech_probe.py --audio TAPE --windows windows.json
python3 scripts/compare_runs.py --old OLD.json --new NEW.json

The talk control is the load-bearing part. The first probe on 7.31.26 returned zero words in all eleven windows, including the ones meant to be conversation. The script refused to let that stand, because a batch with no talk control cannot prove that a zero-word reading means music. A 24 window sweep across the whole tape then found speech at 1:48, and the audit was re-run against it.

Three limits belong with these numbers. Recall of the audit test against each detector's own songs runs 80 to 83 percent, so it under-reports rather than over-reports. The talk control fires the test between 0 and 33 percent depending on tape, which is why every region here was transcribed rather than believed on spectrum alone. And each probe samples 75 seconds from the middle of a region, so a long region is confirmed at its centre rather than end to end.
