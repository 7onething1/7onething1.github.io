The question

One Songsterr tab, one narrow question. Tab s1154774, titled Cosmik Debris Live, is an AI-generated transcription uploaded by DavidxHanes in March 2025. Its drum track writes 1,053 closed hi-hats, 81 open hi-hats and 24 pedal hi-hats. This page asks whether that hi-hat writing describes what the drummer played.

The tab carries its own answer key. Songsterr stores the video each tab is synced to, and s1154774 names three. The first is the reference recording, so the audio was pulled and measured against the notation.

The recording carries usable high-frequency content. Averaged across the whole track the 6–14 kHz band sits 29 to 34 dB below peak, and the hi-hat lives there. A hat verdict is physically possible on this source, which is why the question was worth asking.

Method, stated ahead of the results

  1. Read the tab's notation from Songsterr's own JSON, unauthenticated, at dqsljvtekg760.cloudfront.net/1154774/1540143/<hash>/3.json.
  2. Pull the synced video's audio to WAV, 44.1 kHz.
  3. Separate the drum stem with Demucs htdemucs, so guitar and vocal sibilance stop contaminating the hat band. On the raw mix a hat detector returns 840 onsets in 60 seconds, which is nonsense.
  4. Detect cymbal events from spectral flux in 7–14 kHz on the stem alone.
  5. Separate open from closed by gap-ring ratio: the high-frequency energy still ringing at the end of the gap, divided by this hit's peak. A closed hat is choked by the pedal and dies. An open hat rings into the gap.
  6. Validate every detector against a null, then trust it.
One method was thrown away mid-audit. The first decay measure, time to fall to 35 percent of peak, produced 1,802 of 4,564 events pinned at the 500 ms ceiling. In a shuffle the next hat arrives during the previous one's decay, so that measure was reading the following note. The gap-ring ratio replaced it.

The hi-hat verdict

The tab marks certain blocks as entirely open and others as entirely closed. If those calls carry information, the blocks must differ in the audio. They do not.

Pooled result. The blocks the tab calls closed average a ring ratio of 0.576. The blocks it calls open average 0.565, which is fractionally lower. A Mann-Whitney test for open exceeding closed returns p = 0.80. The open markings do not track anything measurable in the recording. Sweeping the sync offset across sixteen seconds never produced a stable rescue.

The distribution behind that test is worth stating plainly. Ring ratio across 2,081 hat events is unimodal. It does not split into a choked cluster and a ringing cluster. On this source, after separation, closed and open hi-hat are not cleanly separable by any threshold. That is a limit of the recording as much as a finding about the tab, and it is reported here rather than hidden.

Four structural failures that need no alignment

1. The opening is impossible

Bars 1 to 4 write 81 snare hits, 14 crashes and 4 kicks in 11 seconds, with zero hi-hat. The pattern is kick, snare and crash together, then five bare snares, repeated. That is 99 drum notes in 11 seconds.

TAB bars 1-4, claimed:            99 notes in 11.0 s
AUDIO, actual first 11 s:         26 onsets of any kind
AUDIO, densest 11 s anywhere:     64 onsets  (begins t=239 s)

The claim exceeds the densest eleven seconds in the entire performance. No sync offset repairs this, because no such window exists anywhere in the recording. The audio's opening is quieter still: one onset at 0.023 s, then nothing until 2.171 s.

2. Every pedal hi-hat sits in one block

All 24 pedal-hat notes in the tab fall inside bars 129 to 146. The other 141 bars contain none. A drummer's left foot does not switch on for eighteen bars and off for the rest of a song.

3. The open-hat sections are all-or-nothing

Bars 123 to 125 carry 23 open hats and zero closed. Bars 45 to 65 carry 225 closed and zero open. Real shuffle playing mixes them continuously. Blocks this pure are a classifier holding a state.

4. The hat line is thin, and in one place it vanishes withdrawn

This finding is withdrawn. It was measured on a mixed drum stem with an over-triggering detector. The isolated hat lane shows the tab's hat density is correct. See The isolated kit. The table is kept so the retraction can be checked.

Across 30-second windows the tab writes a median of 0.60 hat notes for every cymbal event detected in the audio. Between 349 and 380 seconds it writes five, all of them open, against 176 detected events.

The alignment test, with its nulls

Before any of the above can be called a transcription error, one question has to be settled. Does this tab track this performance at all. The kick was used to answer it, because the kick is the easiest drum to detect and it is independent of the hat question.

The kick detector was validated first. Across 24 windows of 20 seconds, all 24 show a statistically significant kick period, and the median local period of 788 ms agrees with the independent high-frequency autocorrelation estimate of 784 ms. The detector works.

The tab beats its own null by 4.4 points, and that margin is not significant (p = 0.11). Its kick pattern matches the performance about as well as it matches the performance played backwards. A shuffle in reverse is still a shuffle, which is why both score near 70 percent once the clock is forgiven. The tab has the density and the idiom of the music. It does not carry this performance's events.

The global test was run first and was unfair, so it was rerun. The tab holds a flat tempo. The band drifts from 72.6 to 82.4 BPM, and any transcription with a flat clock slides out of a live performance within a minute. The local test lets the offset float freely by up to eight seconds in each fifteen-second window. That is the most generous test available, and the verdict survives it.

The clock, precisely

The tab writes 120 BPM in 12/8 for 135 bars, which is a felt beat of 80 BPM. The performance sits at 76.1. The tab therefore runs about 5 percent fast, and it computes to 460.2 seconds against 486.4 of audio, a 5.4 percent shortfall. Those two figures agree, which is how the scattered short bars are explained: the 9/8 and the three 11/8 bars are dropped beats absorbing accumulated drift. They are not meter.

What exists anywhere else, checked by search

A web search for a Cosmik Debris drum transcription returns commercial sheet music from Hal Leonard's Apostrophe (') book, Virtual Sheet Music, Sheet Music Plus, Sheet Music Direct and Stanton's. Every one of them is guitar tablature. The only dedicated drum transcription any search surfaced is Songsterr's own s412177. So the absence of a second drum transcription of this live performance is a fact about the world, checked beyond Songsterr's catalogue, and it is the reason s1154774 has had no correction applied to it.

The search did settle the drum chair. Ralph Humphrey was in the band from 1973 and played the Cosmik Debris session at Bolic Sound on 1973-05-29. Chester Thompson joined as a second drummer in 1974, so a 1974 live Cosmik Debris can carry two drummers at once. That matters for any future attempt to transcribe this recording, because a dual-drummer stage mix will not reduce to one hi-hat lane.

An independent source on the meter

A German musicological analysis of Apostrophe (') held in the local Zappa drum-source archive gives Cosmik Debris a duration of 4:16 and states its meter as 6/8 and 4/4. It sets out the basic rhythms, the blues schema behind the guitar solo and the strophe-refrain form. Its 6/8 is compatible with notating the shuffle in 12/8, since one 12/8 bar is two of 6/8. Nothing in it supports 9/8 or 11/8 bars.

Provenance, stated carefully. The scan is 08-analysis/zappa-analysis/cosmic-debris7.jpg in ~/Projects/_outputs/zappa-drum-sources/. No page in that archive folder references it, and the live zappa-analysis.com Cosmik Debris pages carry guitar-tab samples credited to KS with material by Andy Aledort, so this German-language scan comes from a separate source the archive did not record. This scan reads here as an unattributed secondary account of the meter. The scan is copyrighted, described here and not reproduced.

The isolated kit, and what it corrects

The verdict above rests on a Demucs drum stem holding the whole kit at once. DrumSep MDX23C splits that into six lanes, kick snare toms hh ride crash, so the hi-hat can be measured on its own. The first 60-second block is analysed here. It covers the tab's bars 1 to 20, which includes the opening this page called impossible.

The detector was calibrated first, then trusted. A threshold set from each stem's own distribution returned 340 kick onsets in 60 seconds, which is 5.7 per second and impossible at 76 BPM. Every figure below uses an absolute threshold, a fixed fraction of that stem's peak, and is quoted as a range across thresholds from 0.04 to 0.30 so the reader can see how much the answer depends on the knob.

Corrected: the hat line is not thin

This page earlier reported a median ratio of 0.60 tab hat notes per detected cymbal event, and called the hat line thin. That ratio came from a detector over-triggering on a mixed stem. On the isolated hat lane the audio gives 0.80 to 2.62 hits per second and the tab writes 2.52. At the measured felt beat of 76.6 BPM a shuffle two-feel, hats on beats one and three of each triplet, is 2.56 per second. The tab's hat density is right. The earlier finding is withdrawn.

The same holds for the snare overall. The tab writes 1.46 hits per second across the whole song and the isolated stem gives 1.18 to 2.03. Only the opening is wrong.

Corrected: the missing hat in bars 1 to 4 was not an error

This page listed "zero hi-hat" among the faults of the opening. On the isolated lane the hat in the first 11.6 seconds is genuinely sparse: RMS 0.010 against 0.026 for the rest of the block, a ratio of 0.39, and 3 detected onsets against 88 across the full 60 seconds. The drummer is barely playing the hat there, so writing none is close to right. The fault in bars 1 to 4 is the snare count alone.

New: the opening under-writes the kick

Bars 1 to 4 give the kick 4 notes. The isolated kick lane carries roughly 27 onsets across the same 11.6 seconds. The opening is wrong in both directions at once, far too much snare and far too little kick, which is what a classifier does when it assigns broadband transient energy to a single lane.

Correction to this page's own first reading. An earlier pass here called the meter map the signature of a beat-tracker losing the shuffle, and called the 4/4 switch at bar 136 a re-grid into straight time. The scan says 4/4 belongs to the song. The switch lands 86 percent of the way through the tab, which is where the scan's form puts the fourth Strophe, the final Refrain and the Schluss. 154 of 159 bars carry a meter the scan supports. Only 5 bars, 3.1 percent, do not.
Those five bars are not scattered. They are bar 2 in 9/8 and bars 4, 5, 7 and 9 in 11/8. Every one sits inside bars 2 to 9, the same opening stretch that carries the impossible snare storm. The meter defect and the note defect are one defect in one place, roughly the first 25 seconds. From bar 10 to the end the meter is clean.

Ghost notes: both tabs miss what the scan writes

The scan's 4/4 Grundrhythmus carries a parenthesised note in every bar, and a parenthesised notehead is a ghost. Measured across the notation, the live tab holds 3,139 notes and zero articulation flags. The studio tab holds 1,582 notes and zero. Neither encodes a ghost, an accent or a staccato anywhere.

Stated fairly, this is not a mark against s1154774 alone. The human-revised studio tab omits the ghost layer as completely. A shuffle without ghost notes on the snare is a thinner document of the part than the scan is, and both Songsterr tabs are that.

What the scan leaves standing

The scan says nothing that softens the four structural failures. Its duration of 4:16 for the studio track sits beside the studio tab's computed 4:19.5, which is another check that the measurement pipeline here is sound. Its short intro, its blues schema and its strophe-refrain form describe an orderly blues shuffle, which is the opposite of a bar carrying 24 sixteenth-note snare hits under a crash on every dotted quarter.

A correction to our own earlier page

The Apostrophe (') page scores s1154774 at +84.1 percent over length, by comparing its 7:40 against the 4:10 studio runtime. The tab is a live tab synced to an 8:06 live video. Measured against the recording it transcribes, 7:40 is 5.4 percent short. The 84 percent figure came from measuring against the wrong recording. The note counts on that page, 3,135 and 1,581, were reproduced exactly here, so the counting pipeline was sound throughout.

Honest limits

  • Open versus closed is not cleanly separable on this source. The ring-ratio distribution is unimodal. The verdict above is that the tab's open markings carry no measurable signal, which is a weaker and different claim than a note-by-note correction.
  • Onset detection undercounts simultaneous notes. A kick struck with a hat is one onset. The density ratios should be read as indicative rather than exact.
  • Demucs leaves artefacts. The stem is far cleaner than the mix, and it is not a close-miked multitrack.
  • Which live performance this is remains unnamed. The upload gives no date or venue, and the tab's description says only "Initial revision". The analysis holds regardless, because the tab is measured against the recording Songsterr itself synced it to.
  • The two alternate synced videos were not pulled. One fetch was made, on the primary video, under the YouTube politeness gate.

What this means for using the tab

For playing along, s1154774 gives a broadly correct shuffle idiom at roughly the right density and close to the right length. For learning what this drummer played, the opening four bars should be discarded outright, and the meter read as 12/8 throughout. The human-revised studio tab s412177 is the better document of the part, at the cost of being a different performance.
