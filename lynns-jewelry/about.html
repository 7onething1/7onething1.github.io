How it started

Four years of repairs
taught the design.

I trained as a silversmith and then took a job repairing other people's jewelry. Snapped chains, cracked solder seams, prongs that let a stone go, clasps that opened in a coat pocket. Four years of that shows you where designs cut corners, and it decides how you build your own.

So the clasps here are fabricated rather than bought. Bezels get burnished all the way round instead of at four points. Solder joints are placed where the metal is thick. Rivets get peened on both sides. None of it shows in a photograph, and all of it is why a piece is still wearable in ten years.

The enamel came later. A commission in 2019 asked for a fox mask in silver and glass, and the kiln has been on most weeks since.

Process

What happens after you order

A made-to-order piece touches the bench five separate times. Here is the whole route.

Day one, the drawing and the sheet

Your order prints out with the size and metal on it. I pull the drawing for that piece, check the size against it, and mark out on sheet or wire from the recycled-content stock. Sawing is done with a 2/0 blade for structure and a 4/0 for pierced work, which is about the thickness of a hair and snaps if you rush.

Day two, forging and soldering

Metal gets hammered, formed over a stake or a mandrel, and annealed between passes so it stops fighting back. Solder goes on in order of melting point, hard first and easy last, so an early joint survives a later one. Bezels are made to the individual stone at this stage, and the stone is checked into the setting before it is soldered down.

Day three to eight, the kiln

Enamel pieces go into the kiln at 800 degrees for around two minutes a coat, three coats deep. Each coat is stoned flat with a carborundum stick under running water, which is the slow part, then washed and dried before the next firing. A rushed coat traps a bubble, and a trapped bubble becomes a pit on the surface, so this stage sets the lead time on the Ink and Enamel pieces.

Day nine, stone setting

Cabochons go into closed bezels, burnished from four opposing points and then all the way round with a rocker. The stone should sit dead level and it should not move under a fingernail. If a bezel is uneven, it comes off and gets made again rather than filed to fit.

Day ten, finishing and the photo

Hand-filed, then sanded through five grits from 400 to 2500, then a satin or bright polish depending on the piece. Oxidised work gets a liver of sulphur bath and is rubbed back on the high points. Then it gets photographed on the bench, the photo goes to you, and it goes into a cotton pouch and a card box.

Care guide

Keeping it right

Handmade metal wants a small amount of attention and no chemicals at all.

Silver

Sterling tarnishes in humid air and much faster against sweat and sunscreen. Wipe it down after a hot day and keep it in the cotton pouch. A polishing cloth handles ordinary tarnish. Dips and liquid cleaners strip oxidised finishes off, so leave them alone.

Brass and copper

Both darken with wear, which most people come to like. A cloth brings back the shine, and lemon juice with salt takes copper right back to bright pink. Brass can leave a temporary green mark on skin in hot weather. That is a reaction with sweat and it washes off.

Enamel

Kiln-fired vitreous enamel is glass fused into metal, so it holds its colour for decades and takes water and soap without trouble. Hairspray and perfume dull the surface. A knock on a hard floor can chip it, and chipped pieces come back here for a re-fire at the cost of postage.

Stones

Agate and carnelian are hard enough for daily wear and still chip under a sharp knock. Take stone pieces off before the gym and before gardening. Never put a set stone in an ultrasonic cleaner, since the vibration works a bezel loose.

Sizing

Getting the fit

Sizing is the one thing worth slowing down for.

Ring sizes

Listings use US numbers. A free paper ring sizer posts out on request before you order, which is more reliable than a string measurement. Fingers run up to half a size larger at the end of a warm day, so measure in the afternoon. One free resize inside the first year on any ring.

Chain lengths

Measured end to end including the clasp. A 45 cm chain reaches the collarbone on most adults, 50 cm hangs just below, and 60 cm falls at the sternum. If you are layering, put 10 cm between chains so they stop tangling.

Cuffs and bracelets

Measure your wrist with a tape and add 1 cm for a bracelet, 1.5 cm for a cuff. Cuffs adjust a small amount by hand, roughly 5 mm either way, and more than that fatigues the metal.
